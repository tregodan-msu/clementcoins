Methodology

How the coins are digitized

Each coin is photographed individually using a high-resolution digital camera mounted on a copy stand. The setup includes directional lighting to highlight inscriptions, relief details, and surface textures. Both the obverse (“heads”) and reverse (“tails”) of each coin are photographed at the same focal length and within the same frame, so that the resulting images can be most easily presented at the same size and scale.

The resulting digital file is stored in TIFF format on primary and back up hard drives. This digital image is then masked to separate the coin from its background visually, and adjusted for clarity and lighting. Then it is saved again as a JPEG file in order to improve the speed of display over the internet. Care is taken to ensure that the name of the digital file matches the number of the coin in the excavation catalog.

Student researchers and interns then transcribe the associated notes—originally recorded by excavation directors and trench supervisors—into a spreadsheet format. These notes may include context information, find location, coin type, or preliminary identification.

Archival integration

The digital records are cross-checked with field notebooks and excavation logs to ensure consistency. Metadata such as excavation year, trench, and descriptive tags are standardized to improve searchability. The final data is uploaded to an online archive, where it can be browsed by excavation context, chronological period, etc. To see the coins by where they were found, choose Find Areas in the menu above.

Backups of both original and edited images are saved to secure cloud storage maintained by Michigan State University and additional drives for preservation purposes.

This methodology reflects an ongoing commitment to stewarding archaeological resources while expanding access to the research community and the public.
